Explore the science

Information Center

Understanding the science behind heatwaves helps us recognise their dangers and make better decisions during extreme temperatures.

Heatwaves are more than just periods of hot weather. They involve atmospheric conditions, environmental factors and biological processes that can affect human health, ecosystems and everyday life. Explore the scientific explanations below to learn how extreme heat develops and why it matters.

Atmospheric science

How Do Heatwaves Form?

Heatwaves often develop when a high-pressure system remains over an area for an extended period. Air gradually sinks under high pressure, warming as it is compressed. This can reduce cloud formation and rainfall, allowing more sunlight to reach the Earth's surface.

As the ground absorbs solar energy, it heats the surrounding air. When these conditions persist for several days, temperatures can rise significantly above normal seasonal levels.

Other factors, including dry soil, warm ocean conditions and changes in atmospheric circulation, can also contribute to extreme heat events.

Temperature and moisture

The Role of Humidity

Air temperature does not tell the whole story of how hot the weather feels. Humidity, which describes the amount of water vapour in the air, also affects how effectively the human body can cool itself.

The human body primarily releases excess heat through sweating. As sweat evaporates from the skin, it removes heat and helps lower body temperature. However, when humidity is high, sweat evaporates more slowly, making natural cooling less effective.

The Heat Index combines air temperature and relative humidity to estimate how hot conditions feel to the human body. This is why a humid day can feel much hotter than a dry day at the same temperature.

Scientific Questions & Answers

Learn more about the physical processes, environmental conditions and health effects associated with extreme heat.

A heatwave is a prolonged period of unusually high temperatures compared with the typical conditions of a particular location and season. There is no single global temperature threshold for defining a heatwave because normal temperatures vary between climates and regions. Heatwaves can last several days or longer and may affect large geographical areas.

High-pressure systems are associated with sinking air. As air descends, it becomes compressed and warms. These systems can also suppress cloud formation and rainfall, allowing more solar radiation to reach the ground. When a high-pressure system remains in place, these conditions can contribute to a prolonged period of extreme heat.

Air temperature measures how hot the surrounding air is, usually in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit. The heat index combines air temperature and relative humidity to estimate how hot the conditions feel to people. Higher humidity reduces the evaporation of sweat, which can make the apparent temperature significantly higher than the measured air temperature.

The hypothalamus, a region of the brain, helps regulate the body's internal temperature. When the body becomes too warm, it triggers processes such as sweating and widening of blood vessels near the skin. Sweating allows heat to escape through evaporation, while increased blood flow near the skin helps transfer heat to the surroundings. These cooling mechanisms become less effective under extreme heat and high humidity.

Heat exhaustion occurs when the body loses excessive water and salts through sweating and struggles to cool itself. Symptoms may include heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, nausea and headaches.

Heatstroke is a much more serious condition in which the body's temperature regulation fails. It can cause confusion, seizures, unconsciousness and damage to vital organs. Heatstroke is a medical emergency requiring immediate medical assistance and rapid cooling.

Urban areas often experience a phenomenon called the Urban Heat Island Effect. Materials such as concrete, asphalt and brick absorb and store large amounts of solar energy during the day. Buildings and roads can then release this stored heat slowly, keeping urban areas warmer, particularly at night. A lack of vegetation also reduces shade and evaporative cooling.

Beyond the basics

More Scientific Information

Extreme heat can affect several systems in the human body. The cardiovascular system must work harder to move blood towards the skin, while excessive sweating can cause the loss of water and electrolytes. Prolonged exposure can also worsen existing cardiovascular and respiratory conditions.

Heatwaves can affect the environment as well. High temperatures increase evaporation, contribute to soil moisture loss and place additional stress on plants and agricultural crops. When combined with low rainfall, extreme heat can worsen drought conditions and increase the risk of wildfires.

Thermoregulation

The biological processes that maintain a relatively stable internal body temperature.

Dehydration

A condition that occurs when the body loses more water than it takes in, affecting normal bodily functions.

Environmental Stress

Extreme temperatures can damage vegetation, reduce soil moisture and place pressure on ecosystems.
